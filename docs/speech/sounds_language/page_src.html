Speech and Audio Processing · Module 2 · page 1

The sounds of language

Speech is a continuous sound wave that never repeats exactly, yet every listener hears it as a string of a few dozen sound categories, the phonemes of their language. This page builds that idea with real Indian-English speech: the discrete sounds (phones) a listener hears in a sentence, the minimal pairs that prove two sounds are different phonemes, and allophones.

Then it sorts the sounds. Consonants are classified by place, manner and voicing, vowels by height, backness and rounding; the IPA chart is that classification drawn as a map, and distinctive features refine it into binary properties. With the chart in hand, a quiz asks which differences count in English and which in Hindi, and a last section contrasts the IPA with ordinary spelling, in English and in Indian scripts.

01 · Speech sounds

A continuous signal, heard as discrete sounds.

Say “ten” five times and record it. The spectrograms will all differ: one is longer, one higher in pitch, one was said with a smile. A child, a woman and a man differ even more, because their vocal tracts have different sizes. In a sentence there are not even gaps between the words. Physically, speech is a continuous, never-repeating stream. Yet every English listener hears “ten” every time, as three sounds in a row, and hears at once that “den” is a different word. To do that, the listener throws away almost all of the variation (speaker, speed, pitch, mood) and keeps a short string of discrete sounds. An actual speech sound, as one speaker produced it at one moment, is called a phone, and it is written in square brackets: [t], [ɛ], [n].

02 · Phonemes

The smallest sound that can change a word.

The listener does not keep every phone apart, only the differences that can turn one word into another. Those categories are the phonemes of a language. Two kinds of unit need two notations. A phone is an actual sound, written in square brackets: [pʰ] is a p with a puff of air. A phoneme is a category of a particular language, written between slashes: English /p/ covers both [pʰ] and [p]. (Spellings go in angle brackets: ⟨ph⟩.) The test that decides whether two sounds are different phonemes is the minimal pair: two words that differ in exactly one sound and mean different things. pin and bin make /p/ and /b/ two English phonemes; pin, pen and pan do the same for /ɪ/, /ɛ/ and /æ/.

The checks show the contrasts are real in his speech, sometimes with a different recipe from American English. His /b/ and /ɡ/ are voiced right through the closure (pre-voiced, as voiced stops are in Hindi), while his /p/ and /k/ start voicing soon after the release. His /v/ in very has almost no hiss: it is closer to the approximant [ʋ] of Hindi व than to an English fricative, and still clearly different from the voiceless /f/ of ferry. His steel/still and men/man vowels differ more in length than in formants (his /æ/ is not lower than his /ɛ/ in these tokens), and /s/ versus /ʃ/ differ in noise centroid by only a few hundred hertz: listen for the difference in the hiss. Contrasts survive; the cues that carry them vary from speaker to speaker.

Phonemes and allophones: one category, several pronunciations

A phoneme is not one sound. It is a category in the listener's mind, and in real speech it comes out in several slightly different ways depending on its neighbours. Each of those ways is an allophone of the phoneme. The surprising part is that native speakers use them all the time without noticing.

Take English /t/. Say these words slowly, with a hand in front of your mouth:

  • top: a clear puff of air after the t, an aspirated [tʰ];
  • stop: no puff after the s, a plain [t];
  • butter in American English: a quick tap of the tongue, almost a d, a flap [ɾ];
  • button in many British accents: no tongue contact at all, a catch in the throat, a glottal stop [ʔ].

Four physically different sounds, and an English listener hears “t” every time. Swap them around (say stop with a puff of air) and the word sounds odd or foreign, but it is still stop. That is the mark of allophones: they are variants of one phoneme, and which one you get is predictable from the context. An allophone rule states the pattern, for example “/t/ is aspirated at the start of a stressed syllable, plain after /s/” (Hayes 2009).

Now compare a language that draws the line in a different place. Hindi keeps the plain and the aspirated stop apart: ताल /t̪aːl/ ‘rhythm’ and थाल /t̪ʰaːl/ ‘plate’ are different words. The same puff of air that English ignores is, in Hindi, the whole difference between two phonemes:

Phonemes are categories; phones are measurable sounds. A minimal pair proves that two sounds are different phonemes of a language, and an allophone rule says which phone a phoneme becomes in which context. Both are properties of a language (or a speaker's variety of it), not of the sounds: the same [pʰ] is an allophone of /p/ in American English and a phoneme of its own in Hindi.
03 · Classifying phonemes

Where, how, and with or without voice.

A language has a few dozen phonemes, and they are not an unordered list. Every speech sound is made by pushing air from the lungs through the vocal tract and shaping it on the way, so sounds can be sorted by how that shaping is done (Ladefoged & Johnson 2015). The first split is between consonants, made by narrowing or closing the tract somewhere, and vowels, made with the tract open and only the shape of the tongue and lips changing. (How the vocal folds and the tract turn air into sound is the subject of A buzz and a tube; you can move a model tongue and lips yourself in Vocal tract, live.)

Consonants: place, manner, voicing

Three questions describe almost any consonant. Where is the tract narrowed (the place of articulation)? How is it narrowed (the manner)? And do the vocal folds vibrate (voicing)? The answers are a consonant's name: /b/ is a voiced bilabial stop, /s/ a voiceless alveolar fricative, Hindi /ɳ/ (ण) a voiced retroflex nasal.

Vowels: height, backness, rounding

Vowels have no closure to locate, so they are described by the position of the tongue and the shape of the lips. Height: is the tongue high in the mouth (close vowels, /i/ of see, /u/ of food) or low with the jaw open (open vowels, /aː/ of Hindi आम)? Backness: is the highest point of the tongue at the front (/i e/), in the centre (/ə/, the first vowel of about, the inherent vowel of Hindi क), or at the back (/u o/)? Rounding: are the lips rounded (/u o/) or spread (/i a/)? So /i/ is a close front unrounded vowel and /u/ a close back rounded one. Two further properties matter in many languages: length (Tamil /a/ vs /aː/) and nasalisation (Hindi माँ /mãː/ ‘mother’). Acoustically, height and backness show up as the first two formants: close vowels have a low \(F_1\), front vowels a high \(F_2\), which is why the minimal-pair cards of section 2 measure \(F_1\) and \(F_2\).

The IPA chart: the classification as a map

To compare sounds across languages, linguists need a script whose symbols mean the same thing everywhere. That is the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA), published by the International Phonetic Association since 1888 (IPA 1999) on one principle: one symbol for each distinctive sound. Its consonant chart is exactly the classification above laid out as a grid: place in the columns, from the lips on the left to the glottis on the right; manner in the rows; and where a cell holds two symbols, the voiceless one comes first and the voiced one second. Hatched cells are articulations judged impossible or unused. Vowels go in a quadrilateral, a stylised side view of the mouth: height vertically, backness horizontally. Distinctive features: the finer classification

Place, manner and voicing already show that phonemes are not unrelated symbols: /p/ and /b/ are made almost the same way, /p/ and /a/ very differently. Phonologists push the idea further by describing each phoneme as a bundle of distinctive features (Jakobson, Fant & Halle 1952; Chomsky & Halle 1968): binary properties such as voice (do the vocal folds vibrate?), labial (are the lips involved?), continuant (does air keep flowing?), nasal or spread glottis (aspiration). In machine-learning terms, a phoneme symbol is a one-hot code, while its feature vector is a hand-made embedding in which similar sounds sit close together. PHOIBLE (Moran & McCloy 2019), the database of phoneme inventories that page 2 explores, stores such a vector, with values +, − or 0 (not applicable), for every phoneme of every inventory. A natural distance is the Hamming distance, the number of features in which two phonemes differ:

Minimal pairs usually differ in one or two features: /p/–/b/ in (voice), /p/–/pʰ/ in (spread glottis), Hindi /t̪/–/ʈ/ in (anterior, distributed), /i/–/ɪ/ in (tense). Speech technology uses the same idea. Classical recognisers model each phone in the context of its neighbours and share data between contexts by asking feature questions (“is the left neighbour a nasal?”); multilingual recognisers map a universal phone set onto each language's inventory (page 2, section 8), and some predict articulatory features directly, which lets them handle a sound they have never seen as a new combination of familiar features.

04 · One phoneme or two?

Hindi splits what English merges, and the other way round.

Section 3 gave the dimensions along which two sounds can differ. Whether a given difference separates words is a fact about a language, and that is why a contrast obvious to one listener can be nearly inaudible to another: English speakers learning Hindi struggle to hear ताल versus टाल (dental vs retroflex, a difference of place), and many Hindi speakers learning English merge vine and wine. UPSID (Maddieson 1984) marks Hindi /ʃ/ and /z/ as marginal (mainly in loanwords), and writes some coronals as s̪|s, “dental or alveolar, undecided”.

05 · Writing sounds down

Spelling is not sound.

The IPA of section 3 was designed so that one symbol stands for one sound, in every language. Ordinary spelling is a separate system, and its relation to the phonemes can be loose. The unit of writing is the grapheme: one letter (⟨b⟩), a group of letters acting together (⟨sh⟩, ⟨ough⟩), or in Indian scripts a consonant letter, a vowel sign or a mark that removes a vowel. English spelling kept many old spellings after the pronunciation changed: ⟨ough⟩ is said differently in though, through, tough, cough and bough; /iː/ is spelt six ways in see, sea, me, key, field, receive; the ⟨k⟩ of knight has been silent for centuries.

Writing systems differ in what their graphemes stand for. Alphabets (Latin, Greek, Cyrillic) write consonants and vowels as separate letters. Abugidas, which include nearly all scripts of India, write each consonant with a built-in vowel: क is ka, a vowel sign (mātrā) changes it (कि ki), and the virāma removes it (क् k). Abjads (Arabic, and the Perso-Arabic script of Urdu) mostly leave vowels unwritten, and logographic scripts (Chinese) write units of meaning. The Brahmi-derived scripts were organised by ancient Indian grammarians who had analysed how sounds are made, so their consonant letters already form the place-and-manner grid of section 3 (Hindi values):

(The “palatal” row is the traditional name; in IPA terms च ज are post-alveolar affricates.) Tamil script, by contrast, has one stop letter per row (க ச ட த ப), matching Tamil's lack of a native voicing contrast.

Indian scripts are therefore much closer to sound than English spelling, but still not a transcription. In Hindi the inherent vowel is often silent (schwa deletion): कमल is written ka-ma-la but said /kəməl/. Assamese writes ⟨স⟩ (an s letter) for the sound /x/, so অসম ‘Assam’ is said /ɔxɔm/. Loan sounds get a dot (nuqtā): ज़ /z/, फ़ /f/. Converting spelling into phonemes is a task of its own, grapheme-to-phoneme conversion (G2P), and every text-to-speech system needs it.

06 · Check your understanding

Six questions.

1. In English, the [pʰ] of pin and the [p] of spin are one phoneme. How would you show that in Hindi they are two?

Find a minimal pair: two Hindi words that differ only in that sound and mean different things, such as पल /pəl/ ‘moment’ and फल /pʰəl/ ‘fruit’. English has no such pair: [pʰ] and [p] appear in different positions and never distinguish words, so they are allophones of one /p/.

2. What are allophones? Give an English example, and explain why the same pair of sounds is two phonemes in Hindi.

Allophones are physically different sounds that a language treats as one phoneme, each used in its own context. English [pʰ] (pin) and [p] (spin) are allophones of /p/: swapping them never changes a word. In Hindi the same difference separates words, पल /pəl/ ‘moment’ vs फल /pʰəl/ ‘fruit’, so there /p/ and /pʰ/ are two phonemes.

3. Describe /b/, /s/, /t͡ʃ/ (chin) and Hindi /ɳ/ (ण) by voicing, place and manner. Which single dimension separates /t/ from /d/, /t/ from /s/, and Hindi /t̪/ from /ʈ/?

/b/: voiced bilabial stop. /s/: voiceless alveolar fricative. /t͡ʃ/: voiceless post-alveolar affricate. /ɳ/: voiced retroflex nasal. /t/–/d/ differ in voicing, /t/–/s/ in manner (stop vs fricative, same place), and /t̪/–/ʈ/ in place (dental vs retroflex, both voiceless stops).

4. Describe the vowels /i/ (see), /u/ (food) and /aː/ (Hindi आम) by height, backness and rounding. Which formant tracks height, and which backness?

/i/: close front unrounded. /u/: close back rounded. /aː/: open, unrounded, long (front to central, depending on the description). Height shows up mainly in \(F_1\) (close vowels have a low \(F_1\), open vowels a high one), backness mainly in \(F_2\) (front vowels have a high \(F_2\), back vowels a low one; lip rounding lowers it further).

5. Using PHOIBLE's features, how far apart are /p/ and /b/, and Hindi /t̪/ and /ʈ/? Which features differ?

/p/–/b/: Hamming distance , only voice (periodicGlottalSource). /t̪/–/ʈ/: , anterior and distributed (tongue tip at the teeth vs curled back behind the gum ridge). Minimal pairs typically differ in one or two features, which is what makes features a compact “embedding” of the phoneme set.

6. How many letters, graphemes and phonemes are there in English though, and in Hindi कमल ‘lotus’?

though: six letters, two graphemes (⟨th⟩ ⟨ough⟩), two phonemes /ð əʊ/. कमल: three consonant letters, each carrying the inherent vowel, but five phonemes /k ə m ə l/, because the final inherent vowel is not pronounced (schwa deletion). Indian scripts are far closer to sound than English spelling, but still not a transcription.

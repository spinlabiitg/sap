Speech and Audio Processing · Module 1

Seeing sound: the spectrogram

A spectrogram is a picture of sound: time runs left to right, frequency bottom to top, and colour shows how much energy there is at each time and frequency. It is the first thing a speech scientist looks at and the input to most speech and audio models today. This page builds one from scratch, shows what its settings do, turns it back into sound, and then uses it to read speech: vowels, hisses, bursts and hums, in a real Indian-English recording.

01 · Why we need it

One picture for when and which frequency.

Here is one sentence, “But life's worth more than cash, she argued.”, 2.99 s long, sampled at 16 kHz. Look at it three ways.

The waveform and the whole-signal spectrum are two extremes. One has perfect time detail and no frequency axis; the other has perfect frequency detail and no time axis. Speech needs something in between, because speech changes all the time. Over the whole ksp recording set (1132 sentences, 35965 phones), the median phone lasts 80 ms, and 80 % of phones last between 25 and 155 ms. Inside a phone, the vocal folds open and close every few milliseconds. A spectrum of the whole sentence averages all of that away.

Cut the signal into short, overlapping slices, short enough that the sound is roughly steady inside each one (a few to a few tens of milliseconds), take the spectrum of every slice, and stand the spectra side by side. That is the short-time Fourier transform (STFT), and its magnitude, drawn as an image, is the spectrogram.
A spectrogram is like a musical score that writes itself: time left to right, pitch bottom to top, and loudness as darkness or colour. The difference is that it shows every frequency, not just the notes, so it also draws noises, bursts and the resonances of the vocal tract.

The idea is older than digital computers. In 1946 Koenig, Dunn and Lacy at Bell Telephone Laboratories described the sound spectrograph, which scanned a recording with a tunable band-pass filter and burnt the output onto paper. A year later Potter, Kopp and Green published Visible Speech, a book that taught people to read words from these pictures. Every spectrogram on this page is computed the digital way, with the FFT.

02 · The STFT, step by step

Frame, window, FFT, magnitude, stack.

The recipe has five steps, and three numbers control it.

Written as one formula, the STFT of a signal \(x[n]\) with a window \(w[n]\) of length \(L\) is

$$X[m,k] \;=\; \sum_{n} x[n]\; w[n - mH]\; e^{-j 2\pi k n / N}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, N-1$$ \(m\) numbers the frames (frame \(m\) starts at sample \(mH\)), \(k\) numbers the frequency bins, and \(N \ge L\) is the FFT size. The window is zero outside \(0 \le n - mH < L\), so each \(X[m,\cdot]\) only sees one slice. Written this way the phase is measured from the start of the signal; many libraries put \(n - mH\) in the exponent instead, which changes the phase of \(X\) but not \(|X|\).

Frame \(m\) sits at time \(t_m = mH/f_s\) and bin \(k\) at frequency \(f_k = k f_s / N\). The spectrogram is the magnitude, usually squared and in decibels:

$$S_{\text{dB}}[m,k] = 20\log_{10}\bigl|X[m,k]\bigr| \;=\; 10\log_{10}\bigl|X[m,k]\bigr|^2$$ The phase \(\angle X[m,k]\) is thrown away for the picture. Section 6 shows what that costs when we want the sound back.

The three numbers: \(L\), \(H\) and \(N\)

Bin spacing is not resolution

A common confusion: if \(N\) sets the bin spacing, why not use a huge FFT and get fine frequency detail for free? You can pad the frame with zeros up to any \(N\). But the zeros add no information. They only interpolate between the points you already had. The ability to tell two nearby frequencies apart, the resolution, is set by how long a stretch of signal you looked at: roughly \(f_s/L\) hertz, times a factor of 1–2 that depends on the window.

03 · Windows and leakage

Why not just cut the signal with scissors?

Cutting a frame out with no window at all is the same as multiplying by a rectangular window: 1 inside, 0 outside. Multiplying in time is convolving in frequency, so every spectral line in the frame is replaced by a copy of the window's own spectrum. A sharp-edged window has a spectrum with tall side lobes, so energy from a strong component spills, or leaks, into bins far away and can bury weak components there. A window that tapers smoothly to zero has much lower side lobes, at the price of a wider main lobe, which blurs nearby frequencies together.

$$w_{\text{Hann}}[n] = 0.5 - 0.5\cos\frac{2\pi n}{L}, \qquad w_{\text{Hamming}}[n] = 0.54 - 0.46\cos\frac{2\pi n}{L}, \qquad 0 \le n < L$$ Hamming is a Hann window lifted onto a small pedestal (it ends at 0.08, not 0). The weights are chosen to cancel the first side lobe, which lowers the highest side lobe but leaves a step at the edges, so its far side lobes decay slowly. (With \(L-1\) in the denominator instead of \(L\) these are the symmetric versions; section 6 explains why the difference matters.)

Measured here on 64-sample symmetric windows (hence null-to-null widths slightly above 4 bins). Harris (1978) gives −13, −31 and −43 dB for long windows; the roll-off rates are his. One bin is \(f_s/L\): 40 Hz for a 25 ms window at 16 kHz.

For speech, Hann and Hamming are both fine and both common: classic speech recognisers used Hamming (HTK, for example), while many newer toolkits default to Hann. The rectangular window is almost never right for a spectrogram. The −6 dB main-lobe width is a fair measure of resolution: with a Hann window two equal components need to be about 2 bins, \(2f_s/L\), apart to show as two peaks.

A window trades leakage (side lobes, which let loud parts spill into quiet regions) against blur (main-lobe width, which merges close frequencies). In a speech spectrogram, where loud low harmonics sit next to formants 40–60 dB weaker, low side lobes matter more than the last bit of sharpness, so we always taper.
04 · Wideband or narrowband

You cannot have sharp time and sharp frequency at once.

A window of \(L\) samples lasts \(\Delta t \approx L/f_s\) seconds and resolves about \(\Delta f \approx f_s/L\) hertz. Their product is a constant: make the window shorter for better timing and the frequency detail gets coarser by the same factor. This is a property of the Fourier transform itself (Gabor's form of the uncertainty principle), not of the FFT:

$$\Delta t \cdot \Delta f \approx \frac{L}{f_s}\cdot\frac{f_s}{L} = 1, \qquad\text{and strictly}\quad \sigma_t\,\sigma_f \ge \frac{1}{4\pi}$$ \(\sigma_t\) and \(\sigma_f\) are the rms durations and bandwidths of the window; a Gaussian window reaches the bound. The exact constant depends on how widths are defined, the trade-off does not.

For voiced speech the natural ruler is the pitch period \(T_0 = 1/F_0\), the time between glottal pulses. From the corpus pitch marks inside the vowels of our sentence, ksp's median period is 7.4 ms, so \(F_0 \approx\) 134 Hz (80 % of the 119 periods lie between 109 and 152 Hz). Compare the window with that period:

Wideband: \(L < T_0\)

A 3–5 ms window sees less than one period. Each frame either contains a glottal pulse or does not, so the picture shows vertical striations, one per pulse. Its frequency blur (about 250 Hz for 4 ms) smears the harmonics together into broad formant bands. Best for formants, bursts and timing.

Narrowband: \(L \gtrsim 2\text{–}3\,T_0\)

A 25–40 ms window spans several periods. The periodicity shows up in frequency as horizontal harmonics at \(F_0, 2F_0, 3F_0, \dots\), each about \(f_s/L\) = 25–40 Hz wide. Timing blurs over the window. Best for pitch and intonation.

Slide the window length

Start at 4 ms in the “cash” zoom and count the striations in 50 ms: there should be about \(50/7.4 \approx 7\). Then slide slowly up. Near 8–12 ms (one period) the stripes fade and the harmonics have not yet appeared. By 25 ms the harmonics are clear. A child or many female voices, with \(F_0\) of 200–300 Hz, would need a window of about 3 ms or less for a true wideband view.

Phoneticians usually read wideband spectrograms, because formants and the timing of bursts and closures carry most of the phonetic information. The classic analogue sound spectrograph offered exactly these two choices: a wide (300 Hz) and a narrow (45 Hz) analysing filter. Speech recognisers and neural models use a compromise, typically 25 ms (section 8).

05 · Decibels and colour

How the numbers become a picture.

A spectrogram is a grid of numbers, \(|X[m,k]|\). To draw it we choose a scale (linear or decibels), a range (which values map to the darkest and brightest colours) and a colour map. Each choice changes what you can see.

Linear or dB, and how many dB

Voiced speech has strong low harmonics and formants 40–60 dB weaker higher up (see why decibels?). On a linear scale only the loudest few components show: in our sentence only 2.0 % of the time–frequency cells exceed a tenth of the peak magnitude. Taking \(20\log_{10}\) compresses the range so that quiet detail becomes visible, and then we must pick a floor: everything more than \(R\) dB below the maximum is drawn in the darkest colour.

Colour maps: why rainbows lie

The eye judges the size of a value mostly by lightness. A good colour map changes lightness steadily from one end to the other, so equal steps in dB look like equal steps. Grey, viridis and magma are designed this way (viridis and magma are “perceptually uniform”: equal steps also look equally different in hue and saturation). The rainbow map jet is not. Its lightness climbs to a peak of \(L^* \approx 96\) in the cyan-to-yellow part, about −25 dB on a 70 dB scale, then falls again to 25 at the top (dark red). So mid-level values look brighter than the loudest ones, and the sharp hue changes at cyan and yellow draw edges that are not in the data.

Whatever the map, always draw the colour bar. Without it a reader cannot tell whether dark blue means −40 dB or −80 dB, or whether the scale is linear at all, and two spectrograms with different ranges cannot be compared.

06 · Going back to sound

Overlap-add, and the missing phase.

With the phase: exact reconstruction

The STFT keeps both magnitude and phase, and with them the signal can be rebuilt exactly. Take the inverse FFT of every column to get the windowed frames back, optionally multiply by a synthesis window \(w_s\), slide each frame back to where it came from, and add. This is overlap-add (OLA), or weighted overlap-add (WOLA) when a synthesis window is used (Allen and Rabiner, 1977):

$$\hat{x}[n] = \frac{\sum_m y_m[n - mH]\; w_s[n - mH]}{\sum_m w[n - mH]\; w_s[n - mH]}, \qquad y_m[\cdot] = \text{IDFT}\bigl(X[m,\cdot]\bigr)$$ If the denominator is the same constant for every \(n\), you can skip the division (or divide by that constant). For plain OLA (\(w_s = 1\)) the condition is COLA, “constant overlap-add”: \(\sum_m w[n-mH] = C\). For WOLA with the same window twice it is \(\sum_m w^2[n-mH] = C\).

The difference between periodic and symmetric is only the denominator, \(L\) or \(L-1\) in the cosine. The periodic form is one exact period of a raised cosine, and copies of it shifted by \(L/2\) or \(L/4\) fill each other's gaps perfectly. Tested on our sentence (47761 samples, \(L = 512\)):

analysis → synthesishoplargest sample error
periodic Hann → plain OLA\(L/2\)\(5.6 \times 10^{-16}\)
periodic Hann → periodic Hann (WOLA), \(\div\, 1.5\)\(L/4\)\(4.4 \times 10^{-16}\)
symmetric Hann → plain OLA\(L/2\)0.0024 (SNR 53.3 dB)

The first two errors are at the level of double-precision rounding (about \(10^{-16}\) for samples of size 1): the STFT is perfectly invertible. The symmetric window's small ripple shows up as a real, if quiet, error. In practice libraries avoid the problem by dividing by the summed windows (the denominator above), which works for any window and hop as long as the windows overlap.

Griffin–Lim: inventing the phase, one round at a time

Why can't we just pair the magnitudes with any phase? Because the frames overlap. With \(H = L/4\), every sample of the signal is described by four different frames. Their phases must agree about what that sample is. Pick phases at random and the four descriptions disagree: when you add the frames back together they partly cancel, and the result's spectrogram is no longer the one you started from. A spectrogram whose frames agree is called consistent: it is the STFT of some real signal. Most arrays of numbers are not.

So the puzzle has two requirements, and each one on its own is easy to meet:

\(\mathcal{A}\): the right magnitudes

All arrays \(S[m,k]\,e^{j\phi[m,k]}\): keep the target magnitudes \(S\), any phase. Easy to reach from any array: replace its magnitudes by \(S\), keep its phase.

\(\mathcal{B}\): consistent spectrograms

All arrays that are the STFT of a real signal. Easy to reach from any array: inverse-STFT it, then STFT the result.

We want an array that lies in both. Griffin and Lim's idea (1984) is to bounce between the two sets: step onto \(\mathcal{B}\), step back onto \(\mathcal{A}\), and repeat. Each step moves to the nearest point of the other set, so the distance between where you are and where you were can only shrink:

The algorithm, step by step

Given: the target magnitudes \(S[m,k]\), the window \(w\) (length \(L\)) and the hop \(H\) used to make them. Wanted: a signal \(y[n]\) with \(|\text{STFT}(y)| \approx S\).

  1. Guess a phase. Start from random phases, uniform on \([-\pi, \pi)\): $$\phi^{(0)}[m,k] \sim \mathcal{U}[-\pi, \pi)$$
  2. Attach it to the magnitudes (we are on \(\mathcal{A}\)): $$X^{(i)}[m,k] = S[m,k]\; e^{\,j\phi^{(i)}[m,k]}$$
  3. Go back to a waveform by overlap-add. Griffin and Lim showed that, of all signals, this one has the STFT closest (in squared error) to \(X^{(i)}\): $$y^{(i)}[n] = \frac{\sum_m w[n - mH]\; \text{IDFT}\bigl(X^{(i)}[m,\cdot]\bigr)[n - mH]}{\sum_m w^2[n - mH]}$$
  4. Analyse it again (we are now on \(\mathcal{B}\): consistent, but the magnitudes have drifted away from \(S\)): $$Z^{(i)}[m,k] = \text{STFT}\bigl(y^{(i)}\bigr)[m,k]$$
  5. Keep the new phase, throw away the new magnitude, and go back to step 2: $$\phi^{(i+1)}[m,k] = \angle\, Z^{(i)}[m,k]$$

Track progress with the spectral convergence \(\;\mathrm{SC}^{(i)} = \bigl\|\,S - |Z^{(i)}|\,\bigr\|_F \,/\, \|S\|_F\), where \(\|\cdot\|_F\) is the square root of the sum of squares over all cells. Stop when it stops falling; the answer is \(y^{(i)}\).

# Griffin-Lim in a few lines (numpy + librosa-style stft/istft)
phase = np.exp(2j * np.pi * np.random.rand(*S.shape))      # step 1
for i in range(n_iter):
    y = istft(S * phase, hop=H, window=w)                    # steps 2-3
    Z = stft(y, hop=H, window=w)                             # step 4
    phase = np.exp(1j * np.angle(Z))                         # step 5
Why it can never get worse. Let \(d^{(i)} = \|Z^{(i)} - X^{(i)}\|_F\) be the gap between the consistent array and the one with the right magnitudes. Step 5 picks the point of \(\mathcal{A}\) nearest to \(Z^{(i)}\) (same phase, magnitudes reset to \(S\)), and step 3 picks the point of \(\mathcal{B}\) nearest to that. A nearest point can only be closer than any other point, including the one you came from, so \(d^{(i+1)} \le d^{(i)}\) at every round. Griffin and Lim proved exactly this. What they could not promise is that the gap reaches zero: it can stall at a local minimum.
Think of each sample as a meeting of the four frames that overlap it. With random phases, each frame argues for a different value and the votes cancel into mush. Overlap-add takes the average, which is a compromise. When each frame then re-reads the phase from that compromise, it moves a little towards agreeing with its neighbours. Small agreements spread from frame to frame, a bit like a crowd's clapping falling into step. After a few dozen rounds the harmonics line up across frames and the voice comes back.

Things to notice: the first few rounds give the biggest jump; the harmonics in the vowels come back as clean horizontal lines over the first few tens of rounds; and the waveform never matches the original sample for sample, even when the spectrogram does, because many waveforms share one spectrogram (the sign of the whole signal, for a start). The numbers from a longer run

Griffin–Lim never quite reaches zero: it is a local search, not every magnitude array is the STFT of any signal, and some differences (an overall sign flip, for one) are invisible in \(|X|\). For decades it was the standard way to hear a modified spectrogram. Today, text-to-speech systems first predict a mel spectrogram (section 8) and then use a neural vocoder (WaveNet, WaveRNN, HiFi-GAN and their successors), a network trained to generate a natural-sounding waveform, phase included, from it.

07 · Reading speech

Phones you can see.

With a wideband spectrogram, and a little practice, you can see the classes of speech sounds. Each class has a typical look because each is made in a typical way (the source–filter model): a voiced buzz or a turbulent hiss as the source, shaped by the resonances of the vocal tract.

Vowels: dark bands and striations

Vowels are voiced and the tract is open, so a wideband view shows vertical glottal striations under dark horizontal formant bands, the resonances \(F_1, F_2, F_3\). \(F_1\) goes up as the jaw opens; \(F_2\) goes up as the tongue moves forward. Open front /æ/ in “cash” has \(F_1 \approx 680\) Hz and \(F_2 \approx 1800\) Hz; close front /i/ in “she” has a low \(F_1\) (\(\approx 330\) Hz) and a high \(F_2\) (\(\approx 2170\) Hz). In a narrowband view the same vowels are stacks of harmonics, and the formants are the regions where the harmonics are strongest.

Diphthongs: formants on the move

A diphthong glides from one vowel to another, so its formants move. In /aɪ/ of “life's” \(F_1\) falls from about 650 to 340 Hz and \(F_2\) rises from about 1410 to 2090 Hz, from an open /a/ towards a close front /ɪ/.

Fricatives: noise, and where it sits

Fricatives are turbulent noise from a narrow constriction, so they look like random speckle with no striations (unless voiced, like /z/ or /v/). The smaller the cavity in front of the constriction, the higher the noise. In ksp's /s/ of “life's” 94 % of the energy above 500 Hz lies above 4 kHz (spectral peak near 4090 Hz). /ʃ/, with lip rounding and a longer front cavity, starts lower: its peak in “she” is near 3220 Hz and only 54 % of its energy is above 4 kHz. /f/ and /θ/ have almost no front cavity: their noise is weak and spread flat; ksp's /f/ in “faced” is 13.7 dB weaker than the /s/.

Stops: silence, a click, then the vowel

A stop is a complete closure, so it shows as a gap: near silence (a faint voice bar at the bottom if the stop is voiced). The release is a burst, a thin vertical line across many frequencies. Then comes the time until the vocal folds start vibrating, the voice onset time (VOT), filled with aspiration noise for aspirated stops. For /k/ in “cash” the closure lasts about 65 ms (22 dB weaker than the following vowel), the burst comes at 1.58 s and voicing starts about 45 ms later; across 97 of ksp's /k/ tokens before a vowel the median VOT is 51 ms. Compare /b/ in “But”, where voicing follows the burst after about 3 ms. The place of articulation shows in the burst's spectrum and in how the formants bend into the next vowel.

Nasals: a low murmur with the top cut off

In a nasal the mouth is closed and sound leaves through the nose. The long nasal tract gives a strong resonance low down (the nasal murmur), and the closed mouth acts as a side branch that traps energy at some frequencies (antiresonances, zeros of the transfer function), so the upper formants are weak. In /m/ of “my might” 100 % of the energy lies below 500 Hz (the following vowel /aɪ/: 23 %), and the 1–3 kHz region is 33 dB weaker than in the vowel. The edges are abrupt, because the lips close and open quickly.

Approximants: vowel-like, but moving

/w, j, ɹ, l/ are made with a narrowing that is too wide for noise, so they look like weak vowels whose formants move into the next sound. /w/ has a low \(F_2\), /j/ a high \(F_2\), and the English /ɹ/ is famous for a very low \(F_3\). In “destroy” ksp's \(F_3\) dips to about 1660 Hz, against about 2820 Hz in the following /ɔɪ/. That dip is weaker in many Indian-English accents, where /r/ is often a tap or a lighter approximant rather than the bunched or retroflex /ɹ/ of American English: across the 300 longest /r/ tokens of ksp the median lowest \(F_3\) is about 2430 Hz, and only 22 % go below 2.2 kHz. Spectrograms show accents too.

Read a speech spectrogram in three passes. Where is the energy in time? Gaps are stop closures or pauses; vertical lines are bursts. Is it periodic or noisy? Striations (or harmonics) mean voicing; speckle means friction. Where is it in frequency? Formant positions name the vowel; the lower edge of the noise names the fricative; a strong low band with a weak top suggests a nasal.

  • Hop. \(H = L/4\) to \(L/2\) is usual. A smaller hop draws a smoother picture and costs more columns; it does not add resolution. For resynthesis, choose a hop that satisfies COLA for your window (section 6).
  • FFT size. The next power of two at or above \(L\). More zero-padding draws a smoother picture without adding detail (Figure 3).
  • Mel. A mel spectrogram keeps the same STFT but sums the power \(|X|^2\) into 40–128 overlapping triangular bands spaced evenly on the mel scale (roughly linear below 1 kHz, logarithmic above), copying the ear's coarser resolution at high frequencies and shrinking, for example, 257 bins to 80 numbers per frame. Its logarithm is the standard input to speech recognisers and vocoders.
09 · Check your understanding

Six questions.

1. At 16 kHz you use 20 ms frames and a 512-point FFT. What is the bin spacing, and can you separate two tones 40 Hz apart? What would you change?

Bin spacing \(16000/512 = 31.25\) Hz. But the resolution is set by \(L = 320\) samples: \(f_s/L = 50\) Hz per bin of the window, and a Hann window needs about 2 such bins (100 Hz) to separate two equal tones. So no. A bigger FFT will not help (Figure 3); a longer window will: about \(2 \times 16000/40 = 800\) samples, 50 ms.

2. A child speaks with \(F_0 = 300\) Hz. What window lengths give a wideband and a narrowband view? Is the usual 25 ms window wideband or narrowband for this child?

\(T_0 = 1/300 \approx 3.3\) ms. Wideband needs \(L < T_0\), say 2 ms (32 samples at 16 kHz). Narrowband needs two or three periods or more, from about 7–10 ms. A 25 ms window covers 7.5 periods: strongly narrowband. For high voices, “standard” settings resolve harmonics, not formants, which is one reason formant tracking is harder for children's speech.

3. A spectrogram of a whistle drawn with a rectangular window shows a bright line with a faint haze above and below it across the whole picture. Where does the haze come from?

Leakage. The rectangular window's spectrum has side lobes starting at about −13.3 dB that fall only 6 dB per octave, so the strong line spreads into every bin. A Hann window's side lobes start at −31.5 dB and fall 18 dB per octave, so the haze disappears into the floor.

4. On a spectrogram drawn with a 40 dB range you cannot find the /f/ of “faced”. Was the /f/ missing?

Probably not. /f/ is weak: about 14 dB below this speaker's /s/, and the /s/ is itself far below the loudest vowel bins. With only 40 dB between the brightest and darkest colours the /f/ falls under the floor. Increase the range to 60–80 dB (Figure 7, and the colour demo).

5. You analyse with numpy's hanning(512), hop 256, and resynthesise by plain overlap-add. The result is close but not exact. Why, and give two fixes.

numpy.hanning is the symmetric Hann window, whose shifted copies at 50 % overlap sum to a constant only approximately (ripple 0.31 %, error SNR 53.3 dB on our sentence). Fix 1: use the periodic Hann (scipy.signal.get_window('hann', 512)), which is COLA at hop \(L/2\). Fix 2: divide the overlap-added output by the summed windows, which makes any window work.

6. After 100 iterations Griffin–Lim reaches a spectral convergence of 0.087, not 0. Give two reasons why it cannot simply recover the original waveform.

(a) Different signals share the same STFT magnitude: at the very least \(x\) and \(-x\), so the original is not the only answer. (b) Griffin–Lim is a local, alternating search started from random phase: each step cannot make things worse, but it can settle in a poor, “phasey” solution that is not globally best. (Also, once a spectrogram has been edited or predicted by a model, it may not be the magnitude of any signal, and then no phase gives zero error.)
